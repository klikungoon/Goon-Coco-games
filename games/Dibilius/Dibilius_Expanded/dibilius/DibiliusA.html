The Dibilius Awards

The award of dumb fails

1. The $125 Million Mars Mistake

Imagine you spent millions of dollars building a spacecraft, sent it all the way to Mars, and then lost it because two teams used different measurement systems. That's what happened to NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999. One engineering team used imperial units, while another used metric units. The software did not properly convert the measurements, so the spacecraft's navigation calculations were wrong. As the orbiter approached Mars, it flew much closer to the planet than planned. The spacecraft was lost, and the mission failed. The total cost of the mission was approximately $125 million.

2. The New Coke Disaster

In 1985, Coca Cola changed the formula of its famous drink and introduced a new version called New Coke. The company was worried because its biggest competitor, Pepsi, was gaining attention. Taste tests suggested that many people preferred Pepsi's sweeter flavor, so Coca-Cola decided to change its own recipe to compete. The problem was that people did not only drink Coca Cola because of its taste. They were attached to its history, its familiar flavor, and the memories associated with it. When New Coke replaced the original formula, customers became furious. The company had underestimated how emotionally attached people were to the original drink. Only 79 days after New Coke was introduced, Coca-Cola brought back the original formula as Coca-Cola Classic.

3. Taco Liberty Bell?

In 1996, Taco Bell announced in newspaper ads that it had purchased the Liberty Bell, one of America's most important historical symbols. Thousands of people reacted with outrage before learning it was an April Fools' Day joke. The company got enormous publicity, but the prank also triggered complaints from people who thought a national treasure had actually been sold.

4. No Beatles!!

In 1962, the Beatles auditioned for Decca Records, hoping to secure a recording contract. The company rejected them, reportedly reasoning that "guitar groups were on their way out." The Beatles later signed with EMI's Parlophone label and became one of the most successful bands in history. Decca's decision became one of the most famous examples of a business failing to recognize future talent.

5. The Bridge forgot how to bridge

London's Millennium Bridge opened in June 2000, but the crowds walking across it caused an unexpected problem: the bridge swayed from side to side. As pedestrians adjusted their steps to keep their balance, they accidentally made the movement worse. The bridge had to close just two days after opening for modifications. A bridge designed for thousands of people had been defeated by people simply walking on it.

6. The $500 Million Button

In 2012, a major online retailer reportedly discovered that changing a checkout button and simplifying the purchase process could dramatically affect sales. The lesson is simple: tiny interface decisions can have enormous consequences. Sometimes the most expensive mistake is assuming a button is too small to matter.

7. The Segway Hype Train

When the Segway personal transporter was unveiled in 2001, it was surrounded by extraordinary predictions. Some people imagined it would transform cities and replace walking as we knew it. It found a niche, but it never became the world-changing revolution that the hype promised.

8. The Titanic's 'Unsinkable' Reputation

The Titanic was widely celebrated for its size and advanced design, and it was often described as practically unsinkable. In April 1912, it struck an iceberg on its maiden voyage and sank. The disaster became a lasting warning against overconfidence and ignoring risk.

9. The Wrong Hole: Mars Edition

NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter was lost in 1999 after a navigation error caused by a mismatch between imperial and metric units. It is a real-life reminder that even brilliant teams can make catastrophic mistakes when systems fail to communicate clearly.

10. The Expensive Window

In 1993, a software presentation became famous after a Windows NT demo crashed with a blue screen in front of an audience. Live demos are risky: one tiny technical failure can steal the spotlight from months of preparation.
